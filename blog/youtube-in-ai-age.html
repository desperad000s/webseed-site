Why YouTube matters more in the AI age

A potential client asked me last month if we were on YouTube before he asked anything about our portfolio.

He'd already gone through the website. He'd read two posts. He'd watched two short clips someone else had shared with him. The portfolio came up later in the call, as a formality. By then he'd already decided the work looked like a fit. The thing he wanted to verify wasn't whether we could ship. It was whether the person he'd hire was the person he'd been watching on the channel.

That call rearranged how I think about YouTube.

I'd been treating it the way most agencies treat it. A marketing output. A funnel asset. A polite content-marketing thing you do because you're supposed to. After that call I started treating it as the brief. The working brief. The strategic document the agency is actually run from. Everything else is downstream of what gets said into a camera.

This piece is what I changed and why. Two parts. What the channel actually is for me now, and what it does to the work.

What the channel is, plainly

It is not a polished agency reel. It is not a thought-leadership monologue. It is not a tutorial series I edit in the third person.

A polished reel is the wrong format because anyone considering working with you in 2026 can spot a reel and discount it on sight. A monologue is the wrong format because the audience does not want me telling them what I think, they want me showing them what I do. A tutorial series in the third person is the wrong format because it strips the operator out of the channel, and the operator is the thing the person on the other side is actually auditing.

The channel is three things, layered.

The documentary. People follow the work. The client project starts, hits a wall, gets ugly, gets fixed, ships. The channel goes along for the ride. I post the broken first try as readily as the final. I do not pretend I knew which way it would go.

The problem-solving in motion. A real brief sits on the desk. I work through it on camera. Sometimes the answer is wrong and we cut to the part where I figure out it was wrong. The audience watches judgment happen instead of reading the conclusion in a tidy listicle.

The market read from the trenches. I take a trend, a tool launch, a competitor move, and run it against what an actual client engagement looks like that week. The point is not that I have a take on the industry. The point is that the take is shaped by yesterday's conversation with a founder who pays my retainer.

Two audiences sit on the other side of the camera at the same time. Peers who want to adapt the workflow to their own studio. Prospective clients who want to see what working with me looks like before they have to ask. I do not switch tone between them. The same episode serves both.

Why this lands with the people on the other side

A bit of context that explains why this works now and didn't a year ago.

Gartner has been tracking B2B buying behaviour for the better part of a decade and consistently reports that around three quarters of B2B clients want to make purchase decisions without speaking to a salesperson when they can (source). They want to do the research first. They want to arrive at the call already confident. They want to verify, not be sold to.

What I see in practice: by the time someone serious books a discovery call, they have already made the shortlist decision. The call is the audit. Anything on the channel that sounds like an audit answer counts for more than anything in a pitch deck.

YouTube is the second-largest search engine on the open web, with around three quarters of consumers active on the platform per Sprout Social's 2025 index (source). For someone considering an agency, the channel is now the first place they look after a name surfaces. If there is nothing to watch, you are being audited on website copy. If there is something to watch and you are the person on camera, the audit is already happening, and you are running it.

Put the two trends together and the shape of the shift becomes obvious. The search bar is turning into a chat box. The video player is turning into the about page. AI assistants are where people start their research now, and the channel is where the operator actually shows up. The homepage still converts the click. The audition that decides whether the click ever happens lives upstream of it.

(Opinion: the agencies that don't get this in the next 18 months are losing pitches they don't even know they were in. Inbound goes quiet for reasons they cannot trace, because the verification step happened on a surface they were not on.)

Why this lands with me as the operator

The effect on the people on the other side is the public-facing reason. The reason I keep doing it is private.

Saying a thing on camera is a discipline. The question "could I explain this brief in 90 seconds to a stranger" is the cleanest pre-filter I have ever found for whether I would take the work. If the answer is "no, it would need a slide deck" then the engagement is going to be murky. If the answer is "yes, here is the spine of it in one sentence" then the engagement has a real shape. The channel is the test rig.

The documentary format keeps me honest. I cannot post only the wins. The audience will smell it. The most-replied episode of the last quarter was one where I rebuilt a homepage three times in a week and the third version was worse than the first. People wrote in to ask what I had learned and what I would do next. That is the contract the format earns: honesty in exchange for trust.

The market-read-in-the-trenches format closes a loop the studio was already running. I read the intelligence brief on Monday. I run an experiment on a client problem. I post the result on the channel. The intelligence loop already existed. The camera turned it into a public artifact someone can quote later.

That last bit is the one I underestimated. Speech that lives on the channel started showing up in answer-engine citations on the studio's own posts. Two of the three early Perplexity citations on our about page came from video transcripts, not blog copy. I wrote about that in the AEO pillar. I underestimated the surface twice. Once as marketing, once as discovery. Both readings were wrong.

What it does to the work

Two things, both compounding.

The channel selects clients before I do. People who come in have already spent twenty minutes watching me think. They arrive knowing the things I would have spent the first month of an engagement teaching. The discovery call is shorter and the work starts earlier.

The act of saying things out loud changes the things. The brief I would write privately is different from the brief I would say to camera. The version I would say to camera is sharper, faster, more honest about trade-offs. So the channel made the briefs better. Not because the camera is magic. Because the audience is real, and the brief has to survive contact with a stranger's attention.

(Opinion: this is the highest-leverage operational change a solo agency can make in 2026. Not because YouTube the platform is special. Because the act of being watchable forces a clarity the agency could not otherwise afford to skip.)

What to do with this, depending on which side of the camera you are on

If you run an agency, or are thinking about starting one. Three things to work out before you turn the camera on. The brief you would be most embarrassed to say out loud is the one to start with. The work you have hidden inside Slack threads is the work to show. The client problem you have solved the most times this year is the episode you wish you had been able to point your last interested client at.

If you are picking an agency. Spend twenty minutes on the channel of any agency you are considering. Watch one documentary episode if they have one. Watch one problem-solving episode. Watch one market commentary. Then ask the founder a question whose answer is already on the channel. If the live answer and the recorded answer match, you have found something rare.
